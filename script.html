Slide 1 · Drop out. Follow your passion. Never give up.

▶ click Drop out of college. ▶ click Follow your passion. ▶ click Never give up. We have all heard this advice. It comes from the founders of Apple, Facebook or Microsoft. pause But here is our question for today: what about the people who followed the same advice, and failed?

Slide 2 · The advice of survivors

We are Group 8, and today we talk about survivorship bias in startup advice: why learning only from winners can make us wrong, and what research says about it.

Slide 3 · Wald's bombers

Let's go back to 1943. The US military studies the bombers that come back from missions. ▶ click The bullet holes are on the wings, the body and the tail. So the obvious idea is to add armour there. A statistician, Abraham Wald, said the opposite. ▶ click Put the armour on the engines. Why? Because the planes hit in the engines never came back. The army was only looking at the survivors.

Slide 4 · Survivorship bias

That is survivorship bias: we draw conclusions only from the cases that passed a selection, and we forget the ones that did not. In business, it is everywhere. ▶ click In the US, about one new business in five closes in its first year, and about half are gone after five years. ▶ click But those founders don't write books or give talks. So the advice we hear comes from a very special sample. David will show how this changes real decisions.

Slide 5 · Study 1 · Everyday econometricians

Thank you, Ilian. The first study is by Barron, Huck and Jehiel, published in 2024 in the American Economic Journal: Microeconomics. They call us 'everyday econometricians', because we all try to learn from data about other people.

Slide 6 · The experiment

They ran an investment game in the lab. Before deciding to invest, players could learn from the results of other people. ▶ click But there is a catch: they only saw the results of the projects that other players chose to go ahead with. ▶ click The bad projects were stopped, so they never appear in the data. The question is simple: do people correct for this selection?

Slide 7 · Result: overoptimism

The answer is no. The researchers found strong selection neglect: players treated the selected data as if it were the full picture, so they became overoptimistic and invested too much. Two details are interesting. ▶ click The bias was stronger when the other players were more sophisticated: they filter more, so the projects we see look even better. ▶ click And it was weaker when the information was more correlated between people. For startups, it means the smarter the people we copy, the more misleading their success can be. Amalia will show you what this looks like.

Slide 9 · Study 2 · What you see is all there is

This is exactly the second study: 'What you see is all there is', by Benjamin Enke, published in 2020 in the Quarterly Journal of Economics. ▶ click Participants had to estimate an unknown number and were paid for accuracy. Some pieces of information were hidden by a rule, and the computer explained that rule to everyone. ▶ click Another group saw all the information on the screen. So both groups had the same information. The only difference was what they could see.

Slide 10 · Result: 60 %

The result is surprising. People were not just a little bit wrong. About sixty percent of participants were either fully rational, or they completely ignored the hidden information. ▶ click For many of them, the missing data simply did not come to mind. ▶ click And when the problem was more complex, more people made this mistake. Startup decisions are complex, so we should expect a lot of this. Andria will show you why warnings are not enough.

Slide 11 · Study 3 · What does not help

Thank you, Amalia. The third study is by López-Pérez, Pintér and Sánchez-Mangas, in 2022, in the Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization. They tested what can reduce this bias in the lab. Three findings. ▶ click First, even when the instructions clearly warned people about the selection, the bias stayed. ▶ click Second, more data did not help: as people saw more of the biased sample, they became more confident, not more careful. ▶ click And third, the bias only disappeared when people knew the exact selection rule.

Slide 12 · Study 4 · The whole economy

The fourth study, by Hirshleifer and Plotkin, published in 2021 in PNAS, looks at the whole economy. In their model, firms copy what seems to work for other firms. But successes are visible, and failures are hidden. ▶ click So firms become too optimistic, and many adopt the same risky project at the same time: an irrational boom. ▶ click These booms can collapse, or they can last forever. ▶ click The authors use this to explain investment booms, waves of mergers and IPOs, and waves of new technologies. Fabián will tell you what we can do about it.

Slide 13 · Three questions

So, what can we do? Research shows that warnings alone are not enough, so we need habits. Think about your own life: an influencer who got rich, an investor who picked the right stock, a friend who dropped out and succeeded. We hear these stories because they ended well. Three questions before taking any success advice. ▶ click One: who is missing? Look for the people who did the same thing and failed. ▶ click Two: what is the base rate? Out of everyone who tried, how many succeeded? ▶ click Three: would this story still be told if it had failed? If not, it is not evidence. It is just a survivor. These three questions take ten seconds, and they work for startups, for investing, and even for the advice you get on social media.

Slide 14 · Look where the holes aren't

Let's go back to Wald's planes. The lesson was not in the holes we could see, but in the holes we could not see. Next time someone tells you to drop out and follow your passion, think of the planes that never came back. ▶ click Thank you. We are happy to take your questions.

Likely questions

1. What is selection neglect? Treating a selected sample as if it were the full picture: ignoring the cases that were filtered out.

2. Is survivorship bias only about business? No: Wald's planes, old buildings ("they built better before": the bad ones collapsed), mutual funds that closed and leave the averages.

3. How did Enke show it causally? Same information in both groups; only whether the hidden signals were visible changed. The difference in beliefs is the neglect.

4. Why are warnings not enough? López-Pérez et al.: the bias stayed even with explicit warnings; it went away only when people knew the exact selection rule.

5. Why does sophistication make it worse (Barron et al.)? Sophisticated players filter their projects more, so the visible results look even better than reality.

6. What is a base rate? The share of all who tried that succeeded. For new US businesses, about half close within five years (BLS).

7. Is the advice of successful founders useless? No, but it is incomplete: compare with people who did the same and failed before calling it a cause of success.

8. Is the 100-startups video real data? The proportions follow U.S. BLS survival rates (≈20 % close in year 1, ≈50 % by year 5, ≈65 % by year 10); the dots are an illustration.
